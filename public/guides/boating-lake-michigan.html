Boating Lake Michigan

By the shouldiboat.com team · Published November 6, 2026

Lake Michigan is long, deep and narrow: roughly 300 miles from Michigan City to the Straits of Mackinac but only about 75 to 120 miles across. That shape decides which winds are dangerous, which shore takes the beating, and why a calm-looking morning can turn on you. Here's how to read it.

Wind direction matters more than wind speed

Waves grow with three things: wind speed, how long it blows, and fetch, the distance of open water it blows across (see wind direction & fetch). On Lake Michigan, fetch depends almost entirely on direction:

  • North or south winds run the length of the lake. A sustained north wind has hundreds of miles to build waves before they reach the southern shore, which is why Michigan City and Chicago can see big, long-period seas from a wind that doesn't sound alarming on paper.
  • East or west winds cross the lake's width, so the waves have less room to grow, but they still build well past what most small boats want in a few hours.

A 15-knot north wind and a 15-knot west wind can make very different days at the same harbor. Each spot page lists the directions that are onshore, offshore and roughest at that launch.

The east shore takes the prevailing wind

The prevailing winds over the lake are westerly, so the Michigan shore, from Michigan City through Holland and Muskegon and on north, is usually the downwind side. Waves arrive at the harbor mouths after crossing the whole lake, and they stack up where the bottom shoals near shore.

The west shore, at Milwaukee, Sheboygan and Chicago, often looks calm in the same westerly because the wind is offshore. That calm is local. A mile or two out the chop builds, and an offshore wind pushes a disabled boat away from land, not toward it.

Pier channels and harbor mouths

Most east-shore harbors are river mouths between two long piers: Holland's channel out of Lake Macatawa, Muskegon's out of Muskegon Lake, and many more. They are the most dangerous few hundred yards of many trips:

  • Waves steepen and break where they meet the shallower entrance, especially in an onshore wind.
  • Water piled against the shore drains back out along the piers, creating strong structural currents that can set a small boat toward the concrete.
  • Wave energy reflects off the pier walls, so the channel can be confused and choppy even when the open lake is merely lumpy.

Look at the channel before you commit, give the pier heads room, and remember you have to come back in through the same entrance, often with the wind and sea behind you, which is harder to handle.

Seiches and meteotsunamis

Lake Michigan's water level can slosh. A seiche is a standing oscillation after a storm or a strong, steady wind. A meteotsunami is a sudden surge driven by a fast-moving line of storms or a pressure jump. In June 1954 a surge of roughly ten feet swept the Chicago lakefront and killed several people fishing from the piers, and smaller events happen most years.

For boaters the practical signs are a fast squall line on radar, water rapidly draining from or flooding a harbor, and unusual currents at a channel entrance. If a line of storms is crossing the lake toward you, get off the water and away from piers and breakwalls.

Cold water, even in summer

Lake Michigan is deep, so it warms slowly and stays cold well into June. A strong offshore wind can also push the warm surface layer away from shore and draw cold water up from below. This is called upwelling, and it can drop the nearshore temperature by 20°F or more overnight. A July day in the 80s does not guarantee warm water. Check the water temperature on the spot page and read cold-water safety before an early- or late-season trip.

The northern bays and the Straits

  • Grand Traverse Bay (Traverse City) splits into two arms that are sheltered from westerlies by the Leelanau and Old Mission peninsulas, but they are open to the north. A north wind funnels down the full length of each arm and piles waves into the south end.
  • Little Traverse Bay (Petoskey, Harbor Springs) opens to the west, so westerlies roll straight in. Harbor Springs, tucked on the north side, is noticeably calmer than the Petoskey waterfront in those conditions.
  • The Straits of Mackinac (Mackinaw City) connect Lake Michigan and Lake Huron. Currents through the Straits can run strongly and change direction, the water stays cold, and freighters use the shipping lanes under the bridge. Plan crossings to Mackinac Island around both the wind and the traffic.

Season and the buoys

Most of the offshore NDBC weather buoys on Lake Michigan are pulled before ice season, typically in mid-to-late fall, and redeployed in spring. While they're out, the wave numbers on spot pages come from the NWS forecast rather than live measurements, and the page says so. Fall is also gale season: the strongest storms of the year cross the lakes from October through December, and a forecast window can close faster than in summer.

The short version: on Lake Michigan, check the wind direction before the wind speed. North and south winds build the biggest seas, the east shore takes the prevailing westerlies, and the harbor channel is often the roughest part of the trip.
