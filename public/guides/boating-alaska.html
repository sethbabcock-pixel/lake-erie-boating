Boating Alaska

By the shouldiboat.com team

Alaska offers some of the most spectacular boating in the world, and some of the least forgiving. Tides are very large, weather in the mountains and fjords can change in minutes, the water is cold in every month, and help can be a long way off. A forecast number only tells part of the story here; self-reliance and conservative planning matter more than anywhere else we cover.

Big tides and strong currents

Alaska has some of the largest tidal ranges in the United States. In Cook Inlet near Anchorage the range is on the order of 30 feet, and the currents are strong enough to shape the whole day's plan. Turnagain Arm can produce a tidal bore, and broad mud flats and fast-moving silty water make grounding and being stranded real risks. In narrow passages and around points, wind against tide builds steep seas. Plan landings and moorings around the tide, since a beach you can land on at high tide may be hundreds of feet from the water at low tide. Use NOAA's tide and current predictions for your exact location and time.

Williwaws and fjord wind

Williwaws are sudden, violent gusts that spill down mountain slopes and out of fjords and valleys. On a day that looks calm in the open water, you can meet gusts far above the forecast average when you pass beneath a glacier valley or steep slope, in places like Prince William Sound, Lynn Canal and the Southeast Alaska passages. Funneling can strengthen wind through narrow channels and passes, such as the area around Whittier and Valdez. Stay alert, reef early, and anchor with extra scope and good holding.

The Gulf of Alaska and open water

The open Gulf of Alaska and exposed outer coast can produce large seas, long-period swell and rapid weather changes, with frequent gales in fall and winter. Crossing open stretches, such as around the Kenai Peninsula, Cape St. Elias, or from Resurrection Bay into the Gulf, takes a boat and a crew capable of handling serious sea conditions. Sheltered inside waters, such as much of the Inside Passage, can be much milder. Check the NWS coastal and offshore waters forecast for your zone (Anchorage, Fairbanks and Juneau offices issue them), and watch for small craft and gale warnings. See Small Craft Advisory, explained.

Glacier ice, debris and fog

Tidewater glaciers calve ice that ranges from small bits to large icebergs, and underwater ice can be hard to see. Keep well back from glacier faces, where calving can create dangerous waves, and avoid running fast in ice-strewn water. Fog and rain can reduce visibility, and driftwood and logs float in some areas. Slow down and keep a good lookout.

Cold water and remoteness

Water temperatures are commonly in the 40s to low 50s Fahrenheit even in summer, so any fall into the water becomes an emergency within minutes. Read the cold-water safety guide, wear a life jacket, and consider an immersion or exposure suit for longer trips.

Much of Alaska's coast has limited cell coverage, thin or absent VHF coverage in some areas, and long response times. Plan accordingly:

  • File a float plan with someone who will actually raise the alarm; see our pre-launch checklist.
  • Carry a marine VHF with DSC, and consider a satellite messenger or a registered 406 MHz EPIRB or PLB for areas where VHF and cell service don't reach. See the VHF guide.
  • Carry extra fuel, food, water, warm clothing, a first-aid kit and spare parts; fuel docks can be far apart. See the safety gear checklist.
  • Be aware of wildlife on shore, including bears, when you land, camp or clean fish.

Use the NOAA Coast Pilot volumes for Alaska (Coast Pilot 8 and 9) and current charts, and talk to local harbormasters and the Coast Guard before a long trip.
