Boating Florida’s Atlantic coast and inlets

By the shouldiboat.com team · Published November 6, 2026

Florida's Atlantic coast is a long line of barrier islands with the Intracoastal Waterway behind them and the ocean in front. To get from one to the other you go through an inlet, and the inlets are where most of the trouble happens. Some are wide, deep ship channels; others are narrow, shoaling cuts with a reputation. Here's how to read them, and what else is different about this coast.

The inlets

Every inlet on this coast has the same basic danger: the tide forces the water of a whole lagoon or river in and out through a narrow gap. When that outgoing current (the ebb) runs against waves coming in from the ocean, it shortens and steepens them into standing, breaking water across the mouth of the inlet. The same inlet can be easy on a slack or incoming tide and dangerous two hours later. The coast guide covers the general technique; on this coast, a few inlets deserve particular respect:

  • Ponce Inlet has a long-standing reputation as one of the most dangerous on the coast, with shifting shoals and breaking seas across the bar when the swell is up.
  • Sebastian Inlet is narrow and between jetties, with strong currents. It is also a popular surf and fishing spot, so watch for people in the water.
  • Jupiter Inlet is narrow and shoals frequently, and the safe path through it can change between dredgings.

The big, deep, regularly dredged ship inlets, such as the St. Johns River entrance at Mayport, Port Canaveral, Palm Beach (Lake Worth) Inlet, Port Everglades at Fort Lauderdale and Government Cut at Miami, are usually more forgiving for depth, but they carry ship traffic and still get rough on a strong ebb against an onshore sea.

For any inlet: check the tide before you go and plan to come back in near slack or on the incoming tide. Look at the inlet from inside before committing. Watch the sets of waves and go in behind the biggest one, and don't cut corners near the jetties, where rocks can be hidden at high water. If it looks bad, it is; wait it out or use another inlet.

The Gulf Stream

Off southeast Florida the Gulf Stream runs north very close to shore, within a few miles of the beach off Palm Beach and Miami, and farther out as you go north. It's warm, fast and full of fish, but it changes the sea state. When the wind has any north in it, it blows against the current and stands the waves up into steep, closely spaced seas that are much rougher than the forecast wind speed suggests. A northerly that makes for a lumpy ride inshore can make the Stream dangerous. Check the offshore forecast, not just the nearshore numbers, before heading out to fish it.

Swell and winter fronts

Unlike the Gulf, this coast is open to ocean swell. In fall and winter, strong high pressure to the north drives days of northeast wind and swell, sometimes called a "northeaster" pattern, which makes the inlets rough and the beaches dangerous. Cold fronts sweep through every week or two in winter, with southerly winds ahead and a shift to northwest or north behind. Swell from distant storms, including hurricanes far out in the Atlantic, can arrive on a sunny, calm day. Each spot page shows wave height and period when they're available; a longer period means more powerful waves at the inlet even when the height looks modest. See how to read a marine forecast.

Summer is calmer at sea but brings daily afternoon thunderstorms, often building over land and drifting toward the coast. See thunderstorms and lightning. Hurricane season runs from June 1 to November 30.

Ships, cruise lines and security zones

Mayport is a Navy base, Port Canaveral handles cruise ships, cargo and launch-related traffic, and Port Everglades and Miami are among the busiest cruise ports in the world. Big ships need the whole channel and can't stop or turn quickly; stay out of their way and cross channels at a right angle.

Federal rules also keep boats away from certain vessels. Around US Navy ships, you must stay at least 100 yards away and slow to minimum speed within 500 yards. The Coast Guard also sets security zones around cruise ships and other vessels in port and in the channels. Watch for patrol boats, listen to VHF Channel 16 and 13, and keep well clear. See VHF radio and calling for help.

The Intracoastal and manatee zones

The Intracoastal Waterway (ICW) runs the length of the coast behind the barrier islands, through lagoons and rivers like the Indian River Lagoon, Lake Worth and Biscayne Bay. It offers sheltered boating when the ocean is rough, but:

  • Manatee protection zones are posted along much of the ICW and its connected waters, with slow-speed and idle-speed limits, some seasonal. Manatees surface to breathe and are badly hurt by boat strikes. Watch the signs, wear polarized glasses, and slow down.
  • The water outside the marked channel is often very shallow, and seagrass beds are easy to damage. Stay in the channel and watch your depth.
  • Many bridges have restricted openings at certain hours. Check bridge schedules before planning a trip that depends on an opening.

Rip currents

Florida's Atlantic beaches see dangerous rip currents, especially with onshore wind and swell. If you anchor off a beach and let people swim, check the surf zone forecast and keep swimmers close to the boat.

The short version: on Florida's Atlantic coast, plan around the tide at the inlet, stay out of the Gulf Stream in a north wind, keep clear of ships and security zones, and slow down for manatees on the Intracoastal.
