Boating Georgia & the Carolinas

By the shouldiboat.com team · Published November 6, 2026

From the Golden Isles of Georgia to the Outer Banks, this stretch of coast is a maze of marsh, tidal creeks, barrier islands and inlets. The ocean side can be rough, but on many days the bigger story is the water moving underneath you: some of the largest tides on the East Coast south of New England, and currents to match.

Georgia: big tides, strong currents

The Georgia coast sits at the back of a wide, curving bight, and that geometry amplifies the tide. Around Savannah and Tybee and Brunswick and St. Simons, the water typically rises and falls seven to nine feet twice a day. Farther north the range shrinks, to roughly five or six feet at Hilton Head and Charleston, and less again in North Carolina.

That much water moving through the sounds and creeks means:

  • Strong currents in the rivers and sounds, often two to three knots or more at peak flow. They change your fuel math, slow a small boat to a crawl, and make docking hard.
  • Creeks that empty. A route that was deep at high tide can be a mud flat at low tide. Know the state of the tide before heading up a creek, and leave enough time to get back out.
  • Wind against current. When a strong current runs against the wind, the chop stands up short and steep, even in protected water. The same sound can be pleasant on one half of the tide cycle and miserable on the other.

Inlets and jetties

Getting to the ocean means crossing an inlet: the Savannah River entrance, Port Royal Sound, Charleston Harbor, Murrells Inlet, Masonboro Inlet at Wrightsville Beach or Beaufort Inlet at Morehead City. Many are framed by stone jetties, and some jetties are partly submerged at high tide, a real hazard if you cut a corner.

The rule is the same as anywhere on the coast: an outgoing tide against an incoming sea makes the worst conditions in an inlet, with steep, breaking waves across the bar. Plan crossings around slack water or the flood, and look at the inlet from inside before committing. The coast guide has more on reading an inlet.

The Outer Banks and Oregon Inlet

North Carolina's Outer Banks bulge far out into the Atlantic, and that exposure changes the rules. Off Cape Hatteras, the shoals of Diamond Shoals extend miles offshore, where the cold Labrador Current and the warm Gulf Stream meet. The area has earned its nickname, the Graveyard of the Atlantic.

Oregon Inlet, the main gateway to the offshore fishing grounds from the northern Outer Banks, has a reputation of its own. Its shoals shift constantly and the marked channel changes with them, so last season's track may now be shallow. Strong tidal currents and an exposed ocean bar make conditions there change quickly. Check recent local notices and buoy positions, cross in daylight on a favorable tide, and don't follow an old track.

The Gulf Stream

The Gulf Stream flows north, fast and warm, and swings closest to the Carolina coast off Cape Hatteras. It draws offshore anglers, but a wind with any north in it blows against the current and stacks the seas into steep, closely spaced waves, far rougher than the wind speed alone suggests. If the forecast calls for a northerly wind of any strength, assume the Stream will be worse than the nearshore numbers on a spot page, and leave it for another day if your boat or crew isn't up to it.

The sounds

Behind the Outer Banks lie Pamlico and Albemarle Sounds, enormous and shallow. Pamlico Sound is roughly 80 miles long, yet much of it is less than 20 feet deep. Like the Gulf's shallow bays, it builds a short, steep chop quickly in a moderate wind, and a long fetch across open water can make crossings rough. A steady wind can also push water from one side of the sound to the other, raising levels on the downwind shore and draining the upwind side; watch for shoals that are normally covered. Bath, on the Pamlico River, is the sound-side launch we cover; see the Carolina Sounds page.

Seasons

  • Fall through spring: nor'easters. Low-pressure systems moving up the coast bring days of strong northeast wind, high water and big surf. They can arrive outside hurricane season and stall for several days.
  • Summer: afternoon storms. Mornings are usually calmest. Sea-breeze thunderstorms build inland in the afternoon and can push back over the sounds and inlets with lightning and sudden gusts. The spot page's hourly timeline shows when the risk rises.
  • June 1 to November 30: hurricane season. The Carolina coast, and the Outer Banks in particular, is one of the most frequently hit stretches of the East Coast. Swell from a distant storm arrives days ahead of it and makes the inlets dangerous even in sunny weather.

The short version: on this coast, check the tide as carefully as the wind. Big tides make strong currents, wind against current stands the water up, inlets are worst on the ebb against the sea, and the Gulf Stream is no place to be in a north wind.
