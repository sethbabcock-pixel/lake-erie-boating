Boating the Oregon Coast

By the shouldiboat.com team

On the Oregon coast the open Pacific is on your doorstep, and nearly every harbor sits behind a river bar. The bar, not the offshore forecast, is often the deciding factor: a day that looks fine at sea can still have breaking waves across the entrance. Add year-round cold water and frequent fog, and this is a coast where careful planning and local knowledge matter.

What a bar is, and why it's dangerous

A bar is the shoal of sand built up where a river meets the ocean. Ocean swell steepens and can break as it crosses the shallow bar, and the danger is greatest when an outgoing (ebb) tide pushes against incoming swell. That makes waves steeper and closer together. Breaking waves can capsize or swamp small boats. Conditions can differ greatly between the harbor, the bar and just outside.

Know the rules before you cross. The Coast Guard can restrict or close a bar to recreational and other uninspected vessels when conditions are hazardous, and NWS forecast offices include bar and hazardous-seas information in their coastal waters products. Restrictions are legally enforceable. Check the current bar status with the Coast Guard station that covers the entrance, and check state rules on life jacket wear when crossing.

The Columbia River Bar

The Columbia River Bar, at the Oregon-Washington border near Astoria, is among the most dangerous bar crossings in the world and is known as the "Graveyard of the Pacific." Powerful river outflow meets Pacific swell over a wide, shifting shoal, and the Coast Guard operates a lifeboat station at Cape Disappointment on the Washington side. Many recreational boaters never cross it and fish or cruise the estuary and river instead. If you do cross, treat it as a serious undertaking: check the current bar conditions and restrictions, cross near slack water or the flood rather than the ebb, and don't go if the conditions are marginal. The Coast Guard's navigation center and local station can tell you current bar status.

Other Oregon bars

Smaller harbors have their own bars, including Tillamook Bay (Garibaldi), Yaquina Bay (Newport), the Siuslaw (Florence), Umpqua (Winchester Bay), Coos Bay, the Rogue (Gold Beach) and the Chetco (Brookings). Each behaves differently depending on tide, swell direction and river flow. Depoe Bay has a very narrow entrance through rock. The NOAA Coast Pilot 7 describes each entrance, and local charter captains and the Coast Guard are the best sources for how a bar is behaving today.

  • Watch the bar for several wave sets before committing. Waves that look fine in one set can be larger in the next.
  • Check swell height and period, not just wave height. Longer periods with the same height carry more energy over a bar.
  • Plan to be back at the bar at a safe stage of the tide, with margin for delays. Coming home can be harder than going out.
  • Everyone aboard should wear a life jacket; tell someone ashore your plan and expected return.

Summer northerlies, winter storms

In summer, persistent northwest winds along the coast build through the afternoon and can produce rough seas, with the best conditions often early in the day. In fall and winter, strong Pacific storms bring large, long-period swell, high wind and sometimes days with no safe bar crossing. A light-wind morning can quickly give way to something rougher, so watch trends in the forecast.

Fog and cold water

Summer fog is common, with ocean upwelling keeping the water cold, often in the 50s Fahrenheit, even in summer. Cold shock and rapid loss of strength are risks if you end up in the water, so wear a life jacket and read the cold-water safety guide. In fog, slow down, sound signals and use radar if you have it.

Shore hazards

Sneaker waves, unusually large waves that run farther up the beach than expected, and floating logs can be dangerous at launch ramps, beaches and jetties. Stay off jetties during big swell.
