Boating Hawaii

By the shouldiboat.com team · Published November 6, 2026

Boating in Hawaii means boating on the open Pacific. There is no continental shelf to slow the swell and few natural harbors; deep water starts close to shore, and the islands' mountains bend and speed up the wind in ways that make one coast calm while another a few miles away is a mess. Here's how the weather works around the islands we cover: Oahu, Maui and the Big Island.

The trade winds

For much of the year, and most consistently in summer, Hawaii's weather is ruled by the trade winds, blowing from the northeast or east. They are often light in the early morning and strengthen through the day as the land heats up. The practical rule is the same as on most coasts: go early, and expect the afternoon to be windier and rougher.

The side of each island facing the trades, the windward side, takes the full wind and sea. The leeward side, sheltered behind the mountains, can be much calmer. That's why the Kona coast of the Big Island, in the lee of its huge volcanoes, has some of the calmest water in the islands on a trade-wind day.

Acceleration zones and the channels

Where the trade winds squeeze around the ends of an island or between two mountains, they speed up. These acceleration zones can blow much harder than the wind a few miles away:

  • The channels between islands are open to the full trade wind and swell, and the wind accelerates through them. The Alenuihaha Channel between Maui and the Big Island and the Kaiwi (Molokai) Channel between Oahu and Molokai are known for rough crossings. Plan any interisland crossing for a light-wind window, early in the day, with a well-found boat.
  • Ma'alaea on Maui sits where the trade winds funnel through the low isthmus between the West Maui Mountains and Haleakala. Ma'alaea Bay is often calm in the morning and very windy by the afternoon.
  • The ends of each island, where the wind wraps around, are often rougher than the middle of the lee coast.

The NWS issues Small Craft Advisories for the channels and windward waters often during strong trades (see Small Craft Advisory, explained).

Swell: north in winter, south in summer

Because there's no shelf to absorb it, swell from distant storms reaches Hawaii's shores with much of its power intact:

  • Winter (roughly October to April): big swell from North Pacific storms hits north- and west-facing shores. These are the famous surf seasons, and they can make north-facing harbor entrances impassable.
  • Summer: smaller but long-period swell from Southern Hemisphere storms reaches south- facing shores, including Honolulu's south shore and parts of Maui and the Kona coast.

Long-period swell carries a lot of energy: a wave that looks modest offshore can break powerfully on a reef or at a harbor mouth. Each spot page shows wave height and period when they're available; see how to read a marine forecast. The NWS issues High Surf Advisories and Warnings when big swell arrives.

Harbor entrances and surf breaks

Many Hawaii harbors are small, man-made basins cut through or behind a reef, with entrance channels that sit right next to surf breaks. Kewalo Basin in Honolulu is one example, with popular surf spots on either side of its channel. When the swell is up, waves can break near or across the entrance. Watch the sets from inside before going out, time your run between them, keep to the channel, and watch for surfers and paddlers in the water nearby.

Kona storms and hurricane season

In winter, the trades sometimes give way to Kona winds, southerly or southwesterly winds brought by low-pressure systems. They flip the usual pattern: normally sheltered leeward harbors and anchorages become exposed, and heavy rain and squalls can follow. If the forecast mentions Kona winds, reconsider any plan that relies on a normally calm leeward spot.

The Central Pacific hurricane season runs from June 1 to November 30. Direct hits are uncommon, but swell and wind from passing storms can reach the islands; follow the Central Pacific Hurricane Center when a system is nearby.

Whales and marine life

Humpback whales spend the winter in Hawaiian waters, roughly from December to April, especially in the shallow waters between Maui, Lanai and Molokai. Federal rules require boats to stay at least 100 yards away from humpback whales in Hawaii. Keep a sharp lookout and slow down in whale season: collisions injure whales and can seriously damage a small boat. Hawaiian monk seals and sea turtles are also protected; give them space on the water and on the beach.

The short version: in Hawaii, go early before the trades build, respect the channels and acceleration zones, check which way the swell is coming from before you plan a harbor entrance, and watch for whales in winter.
