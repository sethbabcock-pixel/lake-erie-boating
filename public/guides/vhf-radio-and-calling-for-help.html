VHF radio and calling for help

By the shouldiboat.com team · Published November 6, 2026

A marine VHF radio is the most important piece of safety gear most boaters don't know how to use. A cell phone can call one number; a VHF reaches the Coast Guard and every boat nearby at the same time, and the nearest help is often another boater a mile away. Here's how to use one, and exactly what to say when something goes wrong.

Why a cell phone isn't enough

  • Coverage: cell service fades a few miles offshore and is patchy along remote coastlines.
  • One-to-one: a phone call reaches one person. A distress call on a VHF is heard by the Coast Guard and by every boat in range, any of which may be closer than the rescuers.
  • Locating you: rescuers can take a direction bearing on a VHF transmission, and a radio with DSC can send your exact position at the push of a button.
  • Weather: a VHF receives NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts with no cell signal needed.

Carry a phone as a backup, in a waterproof case. Make the VHF the primary.

The channels that matter

  • Channel 16: the international distress, safety and calling channel, monitored by the Coast Guard. Use it for emergencies and to hail another station, then move to a working channel. Keep your radio on 16 whenever you're underway.
  • Channel 9: an alternate calling channel for recreational boats in many areas, to take routine traffic off 16.
  • Channel 22A: the Coast Guard's main working channel in the US. They'll announce safety broadcasts on 16 and ask you to switch to 22A to hear them. Some radios need to be set to US channels to use it.
  • Channel 13: bridge-to-bridge navigation between large vessels. Useful to listen to near shipping channels and ports to hear what the big ships plan to do.
  • Channel 70: reserved for digital DSC signals only. Never use it for voice.
  • WX channels: continuous NOAA weather broadcasts, including marine forecasts and warnings for your area.

Local channels for marinas, bridges and locks vary; check your charts and local guides.

DSC and your MMSI number

Most modern VHF radios have Digital Selective Calling (DSC) and a red distress button. Pressing it sends an automatic digital distress alert with your boat's identity and, if the radio has a GPS position, your exact location. That only works if you've done two things first:

  1. Register an MMSI number (Maritime Mobile Service Identity), the nine-digit number that identifies your boat, and program it into the radio. For recreational boats staying in US waters, an MMSI is available free from organizations such as BoatUS. Boats that travel to foreign waters need one through an FCC station license.
  2. Give the radio a GPS position, either from its built-in GPS or by connecting it to your chartplotter. A distress alert without a position tells rescuers who you are but not where.

Without an MMSI, the distress button sends an alert with no identity, and many radios will do nothing useful at all. Setting this up takes a few minutes at the dock and can save hours in an emergency.

How to make a Mayday call

Use Mayday only when there is grave and imminent danger to a person or the boat: sinking, fire, someone overboard, a serious medical emergency. If you have DSC, press and hold the distress button first, then make the voice call on Channel 16:

"Mayday, Mayday, Mayday. This is [boat name, three times]. Mayday, [boat name].
My position is [latitude and longitude, or distance and direction from a known landmark].
[What's wrong: "We are taking on water," "Fire aboard," "Person overboard."]
[Number of people aboard], [whether they're wearing life jackets].
[Boat description: length, type, hull color].
Over."

Speak slowly and clearly, with the microphone a couple of inches from your mouth. Release the button and listen. If nobody answers, repeat the call. Keep a written version of this script near the radio, with blanks to fill in, so anyone aboard can make the call if you're the one in trouble. Make sure at least one other person on board knows how.

Pan-Pan and Sécurité

  • Pan-Pan ("pahn-pahn"), said three times, is for an urgent situation that isn't yet life-threatening: engine failure drifting toward rocks or a shipping channel, a non-critical injury, being lost in fog. Follow it with the same information as a Mayday.
  • Sécurité ("say-cure-ee-tay"), said three times, is a safety message for other boats: a navigation hazard, a large floating log, or your intent to cross a busy channel in fog.

Calling Pan-Pan early, before the situation becomes a Mayday, gives rescuers and nearby boats more time to help.

Range: handheld vs. fixed-mount

VHF signals travel in a line of sight, so range depends mostly on how high the antennas are. A handheld radio, with low power and a short antenna held near the water, may only reach a few miles to another small boat. A fixed-mount radio with full power and an antenna mounted high reaches much farther, and the Coast Guard's antennas on high towers extend your reach to them. A handheld is a good backup, especially in a waterproof, floating model you can keep on your life jacket, but a fixed-mount radio is the better primary.

Good radio habits

  • Keep Channel 16 for distress, safety and calling. Move to a working channel for conversations.
  • Don't do radio checks on Channel 16. Use a working channel or an automated radio-check service where one is available.
  • Listen before you transmit, keep calls short, and never transmit a false distress call; it's a federal crime that diverts rescuers from real emergencies.
  • If you hear a Mayday, stop transmitting, write down the details, and stand by. If you're close and can help safely, do so; if the Coast Guard doesn't respond, relay the call.

The short version: keep a VHF on Channel 16 whenever you're underway, register your MMSI and connect GPS so the distress button works, and post the Mayday script by the radio so anyone aboard can use it.
