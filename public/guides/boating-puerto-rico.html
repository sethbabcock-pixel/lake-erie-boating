Boating Puerto Rico

By the shouldiboat.com team · Published November 6, 2026

Puerto Rico sits on the edge of the open Atlantic, in the path of the trade winds, with coral reefs along much of its coast. Its two sides behave very differently: the north coast faces the ocean and its winter swell head-on, while the east coast around Fajardo is the gateway to the sheltered cays and the islands of Culebra and Vieques. Here's what shapes a day on the water.

The trade winds

For most of the year the wind blows from the east or northeast, often 10 to 20 knots, and it's usually lighter in the early morning. Around the end of the year the trades often strengthen for days or weeks at a time; locals call these the Christmas winds, and they can make open-water crossings rough even under clear skies. As on most trade-wind coasts, the rule is to go early and expect the afternoon to be windier.

The east-facing and north-facing coasts take the wind and sea directly. The south and west coasts are more sheltered from the trades, though they have their own exposure to swell and to storms.

North-coast swell in winter

From roughly late fall through early spring, storms in the North Atlantic send powerful, long-period swell to Puerto Rico's north coast, including San Juan. Because the deep ocean comes close to shore, that swell arrives with much of its energy, and it breaks hard on reefs, rocks and harbor approaches. The NWS issues High Surf Advisories and Warnings when big swell arrives. Each spot page shows wave height and period when they're available; a long period means more powerful waves even when the height looks modest (see how to read a marine forecast).

San Juan harbor

San Juan Bay is a busy commercial and cruise port, entered through a channel past the old fortress of El Morro. Cruise ships, cargo ships and tugs use the channel, and the Coast Guard sets security zones around cruise ships and other vessels. Stay out of the way of large ships, cross the channel at a right angle, and keep well clear of ships at the docks. When a big north swell is running, the seas near the harbor entrance can be very rough; watch the entrance before heading out and think about how you'll get back in.

Fajardo, the cays and the run to Culebra and Vieques

The marinas around Fajardo are the main jumping-off point for the small islands and cays just offshore and for Culebra and Vieques, often called the Spanish Virgin Islands. The nearshore cays can make a good short trip, but the crossings to Culebra and Vieques are open-water runs across passages fully exposed to the trade winds and the Atlantic swell.

  • Plan crossings for a light-wind morning, and check the forecast for the return as well; a crossing that was easy at 8 a.m. can be a long, wet beat into a building sea by mid-afternoon.
  • Wind against the current in the passages steepens the sea.
  • Carry enough fuel for the round trip plus a reserve, a VHF radio (see VHF radio and calling for help), and charts that show the reefs.

Reefs: read the water

Coral reefs and shallow rocky patches lie along much of the coast and around the islands, often outside the marked channels. Navigate by chart and by eye: with the sun high and behind you and polarized glasses, deep water looks dark blue, sand looks light turquoise, and reefs and rocks show as brown or dark patches. Low sun, cloud and chop make the water much harder to read, so avoid running unfamiliar reef areas early, late, or in poor light.

Don't anchor on coral or seagrass. Use mooring buoys where they're provided, or drop the anchor in clear sand (see anchoring basics).

Protected areas

Many of the most popular spots, including several cays and parts of Culebra and Vieques, are nature reserves or wildlife refuges with their own rules on anchoring, landing, fishing and speed. The famous bioluminescent bays are especially fragile and restrict motorized boats and swimming. Rules change, so check the current regulations from the Puerto Rico Department of Natural and Environmental Resources and the relevant federal agencies before you go.

Manatees live in Puerto Rico's coastal waters too; slow down in shallow bays and seagrass areas, and watch for posted speed zones.

Hurricane season and summer storms

The Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 to November 30, and Puerto Rico has been hit hard by major hurricanes. Even storms that pass far away can send big swell. Follow the National Hurricane Center, and have a plan for securing or hauling your boat well before a storm arrives. Tropical waves crossing the Caribbean in summer can bring squalls with sudden gusts and heavy rain; see thunderstorms and lightning.

The short version: in Puerto Rico, go early before the trades build, respect the winter swell on the north coast, plan the Culebra and Vieques crossings around the wind both ways, and read the water for reefs.
