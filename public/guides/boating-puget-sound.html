Boating Puget Sound & the San Juans

By the shouldiboat.com team

Puget Sound is mostly protected water, so wind and waves are often modest. The real hazards are the ones the weather map doesn't show: strong tidal currents, tide rips where current meets wind, commercial and ferry traffic, floating logs, and water that stays cold all year. The open Strait of Juan de Fuca on the west side is a different story, with a long fetch from the Pacific.

Tides and currents come first

Tidal range here is large, and the water moving in and out of narrow passes can run at several knots. Deception Pass, the Tacoma Narrows, Admiralty Inlet and many San Juan channels are known for strong current, eddies and whirlpools at the strongest part of the flood or ebb. Where the current opposes the wind, short steep waves and tide rips can form even on a day with a mild forecast.

  • Look up the current predictions for the pass you plan to run, in NOAA's tidal current tables, and time your transit for slack water or a fair current.
  • Wind against current is the combination to respect. The same breeze can be comfortable with the tide and ugly against it.
  • Small boats should be especially wary of overfalls and standing waves near points and narrow channels. Current is not shown on a wind forecast.

The Strait of Juan de Fuca and open exposure

The Strait runs west to the Pacific and funnels wind and swell along it. In summer a westerly breeze often builds through the afternoon and can reach small-craft strength, with steep waves that grow toward the east end. Ebb current running out against a westerly makes it worse. Check the NWS coastal waters forecast for the Strait specifically rather than the sheltered inland zone, and keep an eye on the Small Craft Advisory status. The same applies to Rosario Strait, Haro Strait and Admiralty Inlet, where open stretches can build more sea than the inland waters nearby.

Ferries, ships and traffic lanes

Washington State Ferries, tugs with tows, container ships and Navy vessels all use these waters. Large vessels can't turn or stop quickly and may be hard to see against shore lights. Stay well clear of ferry terminals and their approaches, cross traffic lanes at right angles and promptly, and keep a sharp lookout in fog. A Vessel Traffic Service operates in the area, and shipping lanes in the Strait of Juan de Fuca and Puget Sound are charted. See the NOAA Coast Pilot for the regulations that apply, and read the VHF guide.

Fog, rain and visibility

Fog is most common in late summer and fall, and low cloud and rain are routine in the cooler months. Visibility can drop fast near the water. Radar, a chartplotter and a working horn matter, along with a plan to slow down and stay out of channels when you can't see.

Logs, debris and kelp

Large floating logs and woody debris are a real hazard, particularly after heavy rain and high water in the rivers and during big tides. They can be partly submerged and hard to spot, especially in low light, so slow down at dusk and after storms. Kelp beds can foul a propeller; shut the engine off before clearing a snag.

Cold water, even in summer

Puget Sound water commonly stays in the 40s to mid-50s Fahrenheit through the year. That is cold enough to cause cold shock and impair swimming within minutes. Wear a life jacket, dress for the water not the air, and read our cold-water safety guide. A VHF radio, a waterproof phone case and a signaling device matter more here because cell coverage can be patchy in the islands.

Crossing into Canada

The San Juans sit next to the Canadian Gulf Islands. If you cross the border, you need the right documents and must follow customs reporting rules for both countries. Check the current requirements with U.S. Customs and Border Protection and the Canada Border Services Agency before you go; they change.
