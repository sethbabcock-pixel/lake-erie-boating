Boating the coast: swell, inlets and the sea breeze

The ocean rides differently from a lake. The waves are usually longer and gentler, but the coast adds hazards a lake doesn't have: inlets that stand up on the tide, a sea breeze that builds every afternoon, and storms that grow fast over warm water. Here's what to watch.

Swell vs. chop: read the period

Coastal forecasts give a sea height and, increasingly, a period: the seconds between waves. On the ocean, the period tells you more about the ride than the height does.

  • Long period (10 seconds or more) means groundswell from a distant storm. The boat rides up and over long, rolling hills. Four or five feet can be comfortable offshore.
  • Short period (6 seconds or less) means local wind waves: steep, close together and slamming. Three feet at five seconds is a rough ride for a small boat.

Many NWS coastal forecasts also break the seas into parts ("wave detail"), for example a swell from the east plus a short wind chop from the northeast. Two wave trains from different directions make a confused, lumpy sea that's harder than either alone. The spot pages here rate ocean seas by height and period; the exact thresholds are in what GO, CAUTION and NO-GO mean.

Inlets: the most dangerous water you'll cross

An inlet concentrates everything. The tide pours through a narrow gap, the bottom shoals at the bar, and swell arrives from offshore. When an outgoing (ebb) tide runs against incoming swell or an onshore wind, waves at the mouth stand up steep and close together, and they can break across the whole channel even when the water outside is moderate.

  • Check the tide before you go, and plan to cross near slack water or on the incoming (flood) tide, especially coming home.
  • Watch the inlet for a few sets before committing. Breaking waves across the channel mean wait, or use another inlet.
  • An offshore day that looks fine can still have a nasty bar. The forecast seas are for open water, not the inlet mouth.
  • If you have to run it, keep the boat square to the waves, stay on the back of a wave, and don't let a breaking wave catch the stern.

The afternoon sea breeze

On sunny days the land heats faster than the water, and air flows in off the sea to replace the rising warm air. Along Florida, the Gulf and the Southeast coast, that means mornings are often the calmest part of the day, and an onshore breeze builds through late morning and peaks in the afternoon. Plan the long run out early, and expect a wetter, choppier ride home.

Thunderstorms and waterspouts

Warm, humid coasts, Florida and the Gulf above all, build afternoon thunderstorms most summer days. They can form and reach you within an hour, bringing lightning, sudden gusts and wind shifts. A Special Marine Warning means a storm with strong winds, hail or waterspouts is affecting or about to affect your waters: get off the water or to safe harbor now. Waterspouts are common around South Florida and the Keys in summer. Give any you see a wide berth.

Hurricane season

The Atlantic hurricane season, which covers the Gulf, Florida and the Southeast coast, runs from June 1 to November 30, and Hawaii's central Pacific season runs over the same months. Long-period swell from a storm hundreds of miles away can reach the coast days before the storm and make inlets dangerous under a blue sky. Tropical storm and hurricane watches read CAUTION here, warnings read NO-GO. Follow the National Hurricane Center and your local NWS office, not just a boating app.

Regional notes

  • Southern California: mornings often start in marine-layer fog, and westerly winds usually build in the afternoon. In fall and winter, Santa Ana winds blow hot, dry and gusty from the northeast, offshore. That makes the water look calm near shore while it pushes small boats out to sea.
  • Hawaii: the northeast trade winds dominate. Leeward harbors can be calm while the channels between islands are rough: the wind and swell funnel between the islands and accelerate. Respect the channel forecast, not the harbor view.
  • The Keys and Biscayne Bay: shallow, clear water hides shoals and seagrass flats. Know the tide, follow the markers, and remember a falling tide can leave you aground.

A coastal planning routine

  1. Check the verdict and the hour-by-hour timeline for your launch, including the sea height and period.
  2. Check the tide and plan your inlet crossings around it.
  3. Read the NWS coastal waters forecast for your zone, including any wave detail and "becoming" wind shift.
  4. Plan to be back before the afternoon sea breeze and thunderstorms build.
  5. File a float plan and run through the pre-launch checklist.
