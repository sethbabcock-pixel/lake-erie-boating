Boating Lake Huron

By the shouldiboat.com team · Published November 6, 2026

Lake Huron is the second largest of the Great Lakes by area, with a long, lightly developed shoreline on the Michigan side and far fewer boats than Lake Michigan or Lake Erie. That's a big part of its appeal. It also means longer distances between harbors and fewer people around if something goes wrong. The launches we cover sit along the Michigan shore, from the St. Clair River north to Thunder Bay.

Which winds matter on this shore

The prevailing winds over the Great Lakes are westerly. On Huron's Michigan shore, a west wind is offshore: the water near the launch looks calm, while the chop builds farther out and the wind pushes a disabled boat away from land. The dangerous directions for this shore are the ones that blow in off the open lake:

  • Northeast to east winds have a long run of open water before they reach the Thumb and the shore at Harbor Beach, and they build waves straight into the harbor approaches.
  • North winds blow down the length of the lake toward its southern end at Port Huron, with plenty of fetch to build a sea.

A forecast of "northeast 15 knots" deserves far more respect on this shore than "west 15 knots". Each spot page lists the directions that are onshore and offshore at that launch; see wind direction & fetch for why it matters.

Saginaw Bay and Tawas

Saginaw Bay, the large bay between the Thumb and the rest of Michigan's Lower Peninsula, is shallow, especially toward its inner end. Like other shallow water, it builds a short, steep chop quickly in a moderate wind. A strong, steady northeast wind can also push water toward the inner bay and raise the level there, then let it slosh back when the wind drops, so levels and currents can be unusual after a blow.

Tawas Bay, on the north side of the mouth of Saginaw Bay, is partly sheltered by Tawas Point, a long sandy hook. The bay is a good option when the open lake is lumpy, but conditions change quickly once you round the point.

Port Huron and the St. Clair River

All of the water flowing out of Lakes Superior, Michigan and Huron leaves through the St. Clair River at Port Huron, and the current at the head of the river, under the Blue Water Bridge, is fast. A small boat heading upstream can make very little headway, and one with engine trouble will be carried downstream quickly, toward bridges, docks and ship traffic. Lake freighters use the river constantly and can't maneuver to avoid you. Know your boat's speed against the current, keep clear of the shipping channel, and have an anchor ready.

The lake just north of the river mouth can also be rough when a north wind blows down the lake against the outflow.

Harbors of refuge

The Michigan shore of Huron has long stretches with few protected harbors. Several harbors along it, including the breakwall-protected harbor at Harbor Beach, exist specifically to give boats somewhere to run when the weather turns. Before a trip along the coast, note the harbors along your route, how far apart they are, and which ones are safe to enter in an onshore wind. Harbor entrances can be the roughest part of a trip in an onshore sea.

Thunder Bay and the shipwrecks

Alpena sits at the head of Thunder Bay, home to the Thunder Bay National Marine Sanctuary, which protects a large collection of historic shipwrecks. Many are in clear, shallow enough water to see from a boat or by snorkeling, and the sanctuary installs mooring buoys at some sites so boats don't have to drop anchor on them. Use the buoys where they exist, don't anchor on a wreck, and leave everything you find where it is; removing artifacts is prohibited. The bay opens to the east, so an easterly wind brings waves straight in.

Cold water and the season

Huron is deep and cold. The water stays cold well into early summer, and offshore winds can trigger upwelling that drops the nearshore temperature sharply in hours. Treat spring and fall trips as cold-water trips; see cold-water safety. If a spot page doesn't show a water temperature, assume it's cold.

Fall brings the strongest storms of the year to the Great Lakes, with gales that can last a day or more. The offshore weather buoys are pulled before ice season, and while they're out, spot pages use the NWS forecast for wind and waves and say so.

The short version: on Lake Huron's Michigan shore, the winds to watch are northeast, east and north, not the westerlies. Respect the St. Clair River current at Port Huron, know your harbors of refuge, and dress for cold water.
