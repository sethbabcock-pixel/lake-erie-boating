Boating the Florida Keys

Clear water, a living coral reef and miles of shallow flats make the Keys unlike anywhere else. They also make them easy to damage and easy to run aground in. Here's how the water is laid out, what the rules protect, and what the winter weather does.

Two sides of the island chain

  • Oceanside (Atlantic): between the islands and the reef runs Hawk Channel, a natural route a few miles wide that most boats follow along the Keys. The reef tract beyond it breaks up some of the open-ocean swell, but Hawk Channel still gets choppy in a steady wind because it's open to the east.
  • Bayside (Florida Bay and the Gulf): very shallow, with grass flats, mud banks and narrow channels. Many areas are only a foot or two deep, so local knowledge and a chart matter more than speed.

The spot pages for Key Largo, Islamorada, Marathon and Key West rate the oceanside water.

Read the water color

In the Keys you navigate as much by eye as by chart. The old rhyme still works, best with the sun high and behind you, and with polarized glasses:

  • Brown, brown, run aground: coral heads or grass close to the surface.
  • White, white, you might: sand shallows; maybe enough water, maybe not.
  • Green, green, nice and clean: usually enough depth for most boats.
  • Blue, blue, sail on through: deep water.

Early morning, late afternoon and overcast skies make the colors hard to read. Slow down when you can't see bottom clearly, and remember a falling tide leaves less water than you crossed on the way out.

The Sanctuary rules that matter on a boat

The waters around the Keys are part of the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary. A few of its rules come up on almost every trip:

  • Don't anchor on coral. Use the mooring buoys at popular reefs, or anchor in sand well clear of the reef.
  • Don't run aground on seagrass. Prop scars take years to heal, and damaging seagrass is a violation. If you touch bottom, stop, tilt the engine up and pole or walk the boat off.
  • Some zones are no-take, and some are closed to entry. Check the Sanctuary's zone maps before fishing or anchoring somewhere new.

Divers and snorkelers

A red flag with a white diagonal stripe (or the blue-and-white "A" flag) means people are in the water. Florida law requires boats to make a reasonable effort to stay at least 300 feet away in open water and 100 feet away in rivers, inlets and channels, and to slow to idle speed if they must pass closer.

Winter weather: cold fronts and the "norther"

From November through March, cold fronts reach the Keys every week or two. Ahead of a front the wind clocks to the south and southwest; behind it, a strong north to northeast wind can blow for a day or more. That turns Hawk Channel into a short, steep chop even under a blue sky. The window right before a front, or once the wind eases after one, is often the best boating of the week. Watch each spot page's week ahead.

In summer the pattern flips: light mornings, afternoon thunderstorms, and waterspouts, which are common around the Keys. Give any you see a wide berth.

Currents under the bridges

The tidal range in the Keys is small, but the water moving between the Atlantic and the bay squeezes through the gaps between islands. Current under the bridges can run hard enough to push a small boat sideways into a piling. Approach bridge channels slowly and square to the current.
