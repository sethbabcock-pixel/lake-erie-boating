Man overboard: prevention and recovery

By the shouldiboat.com team · Published November 6, 2026

Someone falling overboard is one of the most common serious emergencies on a small boat, and the first minute decides how it ends. A person in the water disappears among the waves surprisingly fast, cold water takes away their strength quickly, and the boat itself, with its propeller, can be the biggest danger to them. Here's what to do, and how to keep it from happening.

The first ten seconds

  1. Shout "Man overboard!" and which side, so everyone aboard knows.
  2. Point, and keep pointing. Assign one person to do nothing but watch the person in the water and point at them continuously. Once you lose sight of a head in waves, it is very hard to find again.
  3. Throw something that floats: a throwable cushion or ring, a life jacket, a fender. It gives them something to hold and marks the spot.
  4. Mark the position. Most chartplotters and GPS units have a MOB button that records the exact spot and steers you back. Press it.
  5. Get the propeller away from them. If they fell off the side, turn toward that side so the stern and prop swing away from the person, and slow down.

Getting back to them

Turn around and return at a moderate, controlled speed, keeping the person in sight. On a small powerboat a tight turn back toward them is usually quickest. Larger boats, or boats that have lost sight of the person, may use a Williamson turn (turn hard to one side about 60 degrees off the original course, then hard the other way until you're heading back down your own track) to come back along the path you came.

If you can't find them, can't get back quickly, or they're hurt, call for help right away on VHF Channel 16 with a Mayday (see VHF radio and calling for help), or press your radio's DSC distress button. Don't wait until you've searched for a while.

The approach

  • Approach slowly, and generally head into the wind or waves as you come up to them, so you can control the boat and it doesn't drift down on top of them.
  • Stop close to them, alongside, and put the engine in neutral. Turn the engine off before they're anywhere near the stern. Never approach with the propeller turning near a person in the water.
  • If you can't get close safely, throw a line or a throwable with a line attached and pull them in.
  • Don't jump in after them unless it's the last resort and you're wearing a life jacket and attached to the boat by a line. A second person in the water often makes two victims.

Getting them back aboard

This is often harder than expected. A person in wet clothes is heavy, and after even a few minutes in cold water their hands and arms may barely work. Plan for it before you need it:

  • A boarding ladder that someone in the water can reach and deploy themselves is invaluable, but only with the engine off.
  • A loop of line, or a sling, around the person can help two people pull them up. Use the boat's lowest point, and keep the boat's weight balanced so you don't capsize it by having everyone lean over one side.
  • Once aboard, treat them gently; someone who has been in cold water may be hypothermic, should be kept warm and still, and may need medical attention even if they seem fine.

In cold water, the clock is short. See cold-water safety for what cold shock and the 1-10-1 rule mean for both the person in the water and the rescue.

Prevention

  • Wear the life jacket. A life jacket in a locker does nothing for someone who goes overboard unexpectedly, and that is exactly how most people go in. Inflatable life jackets are comfortable enough to wear all day.
  • Use the engine cut-off switch. If your boat has an engine cut-off switch (the lanyard or wireless "kill switch"), attach it to the operator. Federal rules now require it on many boats under 26 feet when running on plane or above displacement speed. If the operator goes overboard, the engine stops and the boat doesn't circle back over them.
  • Stay seated and low when underway in chop, keep three points of contact moving around the boat, and don't sit on gunwales, bows or seat backs while moving.
  • Slow down before turning, and warn passengers before you change speed or direction.
  • Relieving oneself over the side, especially at night or after drinking, is a classic cause of falls overboard. Avoid alcohol while operating.
  • Practice. Throw a fender overboard on a calm day and run the whole drill, including getting a heavy object back aboard. Make sure someone else on the boat knows how to stop it and use the radio.

The short version: shout, point, throw, press MOB, and swing the propeller away. Come back slowly into the wind, engine off before they're near the stern, and call for help early. Better still: wear life jackets and clip on the kill switch.
