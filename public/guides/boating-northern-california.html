Boating San Francisco Bay & Northern California

By the shouldiboat.com team

Northern California has two very different boating worlds. Inside San Francisco Bay you get one of the most reliable summer sea breezes anywhere, plus strong tidal currents at the Golden Gate. Outside, the coast north of the Bay is steep, foggy, cold and exposed to northwest wind and swell, with few protected harbors. Both reward early starts and respect for the tide.

The afternoon sea breeze on the Bay

On summer days, heat in the Central Valley pulls cool ocean air through the Golden Gate and over the Bay. Mornings are often calm or light, then a west to southwest breeze builds through the afternoon, and in the central Bay and near the Gate it commonly reaches 20 knots or more. The Bay is shallow in many places, so the chop becomes short and steep, especially against an ebb. The simplest plan is to be on the water early and be heading back to your slip by early afternoon. Watch for Small Craft Advisories in the NWS forecast for the San Francisco Bay zone.

The Golden Gate: ebb tide and the bar

A large volume of water moves through the Golden Gate on every tide. A strong ebb running out against ocean swell and westerly wind can steepen seas sharply at the entrance and over the shoals just outside the Gate (the area boaters call the "Potato Patch" on the north side). Large waves can break out there. The current inside and near the bridge can run several knots, with eddies and boils.

  • Check the NOAA current predictions and plan your passage for slack or a flood, not a strong ebb.
  • Check the swell height and period in the NWS coastal waters forecast, and the NDBC buoy reports offshore. Big long-period swell and an ebb is the combination to avoid.
  • Pay attention to the Coast Guard and NWS: hazardous bar and surf information can be issued for the entrance.
  • Shipping channels are busy with large vessels, tugs and ferries. Stay out of the main channel when you can, and keep clear of vessels that can't maneuver.

Fog

Summer fog regularly covers the Gate, the outer coast and much of the Bay in the morning and evening. Radar, a chartplotter, AIS and a horn are valuable. Fog plus ship traffic plus current is a demanding mix; if you can't see, slow down and keep out of the shipping lanes.

The coast north of the Bay

North of Point Reyes the coast is rugged, with few harbors and no easy shelter. Strong northwest winds are common, especially in spring and summer, and they accelerate around headlands such as Point Reyes, Point Arena and Cape Mendocino. Seas can be much larger a few miles offshore than in a harbor. Harbors such as Bodega Bay, Noyo River (Fort Bragg), Shelter Cove and Eureka are far apart, so plan your fuel, your weather window and your fallback options. Early morning is generally the calmest part of the day.

Humboldt Bar and other bars

The entrance to Humboldt Bay, at Eureka, is a well-known bar crossing where swell breaks across the entrance, and it has a history of serious accidents. The Coast Guard may restrict the bar to recreational boats when conditions are rough, and NWS forecast offices post bar and hazardous-seas information. Other entrances, such as the Noyo and Crescent City, also need caution at the wrong tide and swell. Read the Oregon coast guide's section on bars, which applies here too, and check with the Coast Guard before crossing.

Cold water

Water off the coast is commonly in the 50s Fahrenheit year-round because of upwelling, with the Bay a little warmer in late summer. Cold shock and rapid incapacitation are real risks even on a sunny day. Wear a life jacket and read the cold-water safety guide.
