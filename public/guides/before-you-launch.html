Before you launch

Most bad days on the water trace back to something skipped on the ramp. This five-minute routine — conditions, float plan, boat, fuel, comms — catches the big ones.

1. Check the conditions — and the whole day

Start with the current verdict for your launch, then look past this hour. Is the wind building or easing? Is there a "be back in by" time on the timeline? Does an afternoon storm or wind shift turn a fine morning sour? The Great Lakes reward boaters who plan their return before they leave. Check the water temperature too — a warm day over cold water is still a cold-water day.

  • Current verdict and wind/wave numbers for your spot
  • The hour-by-hour trend and any "head in by" time
  • Active marine warnings (see SCAs explained)
  • Wind direction for your shoreline (why it matters)

2. File a float plan

A float plan is simply telling a reliable person ashore where you're going and when you'll be back — so that if you don't return, someone knows to raise the alarm and where to point the search. It takes two minutes and it's the single most valuable safety step that costs nothing. Include:

  • Who's aboard (names, and a contact for each)
  • Your boat — make, color, length, registration number
  • Where you're launching, roughly where you're headed, and your planned return time
  • Your vehicle and trailer description and where they're parked
  • What to do, and who to call (the Coast Guard), if you're overdue

Then close the loop: tell that person when you're safely back, so nobody launches a rescue for a boat that's already on the trailer.

3. The boat and trailer

  • Drain plug in. The classic, humbling mistake — confirm it's seated before you back down the ramp.
  • Battery, bilge and blower — battery on, bilge dry, engine-compartment blower run before starting a gas inboard.
  • Engine starts and cooling water flows (telltale stream) before you leave the dock.
  • Steering, throttle and shifter move freely.
  • Trailer — lights, tires (including the spare), and hitch/safety chains before you even leave home.

4. Fuel and range

Run the rule of thirds: a third of your fuel to get out, a third to get back, and a third held in reserve for wind, current, or a longer way home. Know your boat's real range, not the optimistic one, and top off if the day might turn and push you around.

5. Communication and safety gear

  • VHF radio on and working; know that Channel 16 is for hailing and distress.
  • Phone charged and in a waterproof case, as a backup.
  • Life jackets aboard for everyone and worn — kids always, everyone in cold water or rough conditions.
  • Run the full safety-gear checklist at least at the start of the season, and spot-check it each trip.

6. Brief your crew

Even a casual crew should know three things: where the life jackets and fire extinguisher are, how to call for help on the VHF, and how to turn the boat around and slow down if the skipper goes overboard. Thirty seconds of briefing turns passengers into a backup.

The two-minute version: Conditions checked and return planned · someone ashore knows the plan · plug in, engine runs, fuel enough · VHF and phone working · PFDs on · crew briefed. If all six are true, cast off.
