Boating the Northeast: Long Island Sound, NY/NJ & New England

By the shouldiboat.com team

The Northeast packs a lot of different water into a short stretch of coast: the long, sheltered Sound, the crowded harbor at New York, the surf-line inlets of New Jersey, and the fog-prone, rocky, tidal waters of New England. The common threads are strong currents in the narrow passages, summer thunderstorms and fog, and water that stays cold well into summer.

Long Island Sound

The Sound is about 100 miles long and fairly protected from ocean swell, but wind can build a short steep chop over its length, especially a strong westerly or easterly blowing down the long axis. Summer southwesterly sea breezes commonly freshen in the afternoon. The big hazard is thunderstorms: they build quickly on hot, humid afternoons, and a squall line can cross the Sound with sudden strong gusts. Check the radar and the NWS marine forecast, and read our thunderstorm guide.

The eastern end has strong tidal currents. The Race, Plum Gut and the channels between the islands can run several knots, and wind against current builds steep rips. Further west, the East River, including Hell Gate, has strong, tricky currents and heavy traffic. Time your passage by the NOAA current predictions.

New York Harbor and the New Jersey coast

New York Harbor handles ferries, tugs and barges, container ships and tankers. Wakes are heavy and large vessels can't stop or turn quickly. Stay out of the channels when possible, understand security zones and keep a sharp lookout. The Atlantic-facing New Jersey coast has inlets (Sandy Hook, Manasquan, Barnegat and others) where ebb tides running against onshore swell steepen the waves. Check the inlet before going out and coming back, as in our coast guide. Shoals shift, so use current charts and local advice. Nor'easters and other fall and winter storms bring big seas and long periods when the inlets are unsafe.

Buzzards Bay and the Cape

On summer days, heating over land pulls a thermal southwesterly into Buzzards Bay that typically builds through the afternoon and can reach 15 to 25 knots, producing a steep, short chop in the funnel-shaped bay. Mornings are usually gentler. The Cape Cod Canal has a strong current that can reach around four knots or more and changes direction with the tide; check the current times and follow the canal regulations. Nantucket Sound and the waters off Cape Cod have shifting shoals, strong tidal flow and rips, and offshore weather can be very different from the harbors.

Fog, lobster pots and ledges

Fog is common along the Northeast coast, especially in late spring and summer when warm air moves over cold water, and it's thick in the Gulf of Maine. Radar, a chartplotter and a horn help, but speed matters most. Lobster and crab pots litter the coastal waters in New England and can foul a propeller, so watch for floats and avoid running through marked pot fields at speed. Rocks and ledges are numerous; stay in the marked channels.

Tides, hurricanes and cold water

Tidal range grows as you go north, from a few feet on Long Island Sound to roughly ten feet or more in much of coastal Maine, and ramps and moorings change with it. Hurricane and tropical storm season runs June through November, with peak risk typically late summer into fall. Water temperatures often remain in the 50s Fahrenheit through early summer, colder in Maine, so the cold-water safety guide applies for much of the boating season.
