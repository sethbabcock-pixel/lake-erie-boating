Thunderstorms and lightning on the water

By the shouldiboat.com team · Published November 6, 2026

On a summer day the most dangerous weather you're likely to meet on the water isn't a gale that builds over hours. It's a thunderstorm that goes from a distant rumble to sudden wind, blinding rain and lightning in a matter of minutes. The good news is that most storms give warning, if you know where to look. The best plan is simple: don't be out there when one arrives.

Before you go: the forecast

Check the NWS marine forecast and the hourly forecast before you launch. Phrases like "a chance of thunderstorms in the afternoon," "some storms may be severe" or "locally higher winds and waves in and near thunderstorms" are your cue to plan a short trip, stay close to the ramp, or go out early and be back before the storms build. On many coasts and lakes, summer storms follow a daily rhythm: calm mornings, clouds building over land around midday, storms in the afternoon or evening. Each spot page's hour-by-hour timeline shows when the risk rises.

On the water: watch the sky and the radar

  • Tall, towering clouds building quickly, especially with a dark, flat base or an anvil-shaped top, are developing storms.
  • A dark line or a low, rolling "shelf" cloud approaching is the leading edge of a storm, and the wind is right behind it.
  • Thunder means you are within range of lightning. If you can hear it, it's time to head in.
  • Radar: a weather app with live radar on your phone shows where storms are and which way they're moving. Check it regularly on any day with storms in the forecast.
  • Your VHF: the NWS issues Special Marine Warnings for storms capable of very strong wind gusts, large hail or waterspouts over the water. They're broadcast on the VHF weather channels and announced by the Coast Guard. See VHF radio and calling for help.

Storms often move faster than they look. Leave early enough to beat one to the dock, remembering that you may be heading into a building chop.

The gust front hits before the rain

Air rushing down and out of a thunderstorm spreads ahead of it as a gust front. It often arrives a few minutes before the rain, sometimes from a completely different direction from the wind you've had all day, and it can go from light air to very strong gusts in under a minute. That sudden shift catches anchored boats, sailboats with full sail up, and small boats broadside to the new waves. A sudden drop in temperature and a cool blast of wind mean the storm is nearly on you.

Lightning

On the water, a boat is often the tallest object for a long way around, and there is nowhere on a small open boat that is safe from lightning. Lightning can strike miles from the storm itself, including ahead of the rain and after it seems to have passed. The only safe choice is to be off the water and inside a building or a hard-topped vehicle before the storm arrives.

If you can't get in before it hits:

  • Get into a cabin if your boat has one, and stay in the center of it.
  • On an open boat, crouch low in the middle of the boat, away from the mast, antennas, rails, towers and other metal.
  • Don't touch metal fittings or electrical equipment, and keep out of the water; don't swim or hang over the side.
  • Lower or remove tall antennas, outriggers and fishing rods if you can do so safely before the storm arrives.

Larger boats can be fitted with a lightning protection system that gives the strike a path to the water. It reduces the risk to the crew and boat but doesn't eliminate it.

If the storm catches you

  1. Everyone into life jackets, before the wind hits.
  2. Secure loose gear and close hatches. Get everyone low and in the boat.
  3. Note your position on the chartplotter, and know which way open water lies; visibility may drop to almost nothing in the rain.
  4. Head into the wind and waves at an angle, at the slowest speed that keeps steering control. Taking big waves on the beam risks swamping or capsize.
  5. Stay away from shore, rocks and other hazards if you can't safely reach a harbor. A sudden wind shift can turn a sheltering shore into a lee shore.
  6. Turn on your navigation lights so other boats can see you, and keep a lookout.

Most thunderstorms pass in under an hour. Once the wind and waves settle enough to run safely, head in without lingering, since lightning can continue after the rain ends, and watch for a second storm behind the first.

Waterspouts

Waterspouts are rotating columns of wind over the water, common in summer around Florida and the Gulf and also seen on the Great Lakes, especially in late summer and fall. Many are relatively weak, but they can still capsize a small boat, and some are tornadoes that have moved over the water. If you see one, don't go closer to look. Move away at a right angle to its direction of travel.

The short version: check the forecast and plan to be off the water before afternoon storms. Watch the sky, the radar and the VHF. If you hear thunder, head in. If a storm catches you, life jackets on, everyone low, and head slowly into the waves.
