Anchoring basics

By the shouldiboat.com team · Published November 6, 2026

Anchoring is how you stop for lunch, fish a spot, swim, or ride out an engine problem without drifting onto the rocks. Done well, it's quick and secure. Done badly, it's how boats end up dragging through an anchorage at night or swamped by a wave over the stern. Here's how to do it right.

Pick the spot

  • Shelter: look for a spot protected from the wind and waves you have now and the ones in the forecast. Check whether the wind is expected to shift; a cove that's calm in a west wind can be dangerous in an east wind. See wind direction & fetch.
  • Depth: note the depth on your chart or sounder. You'll need it to work out how much line to let out, and in tidal water allow for the tide rising and falling while you're there.
  • The bottom: sand and firm mud hold well. Soft mud, weed, grass and rock hold poorly. The chart often marks the bottom type.
  • What to avoid: channels and fairways, cable and pipeline areas marked on the chart, coral and seagrass (often protected, and easily destroyed), and spots too close to other boats or a lee shore.

The right anchor

  • Fluke (Danforth-type) anchors are light and hold very well in sand and mud. They can struggle to dig into weed, grass or hard bottoms, and may break out if the wind swings around.
  • Plow, claw and modern scoop-type anchors set in a wider range of bottoms and tend to reset if the boat swings.
  • Grapnel anchors are for rocky bottoms or small boats stopping briefly, and they often snag.

A few feet of chain between the anchor and the rope helps the anchor lie flat and dig in, and protects the rope from chafe on the bottom. Size the anchor for your boat using the manufacturer's chart, and go up a size if in doubt.

Scope: how much line to let out

An anchor holds best when the pull on it is close to horizontal, which means letting out much more line (the rode) than the depth of the water. The ratio of rode to depth is called scope. Measure depth from your bow, not the waterline, and add any expected rise in tide.

  • With a rope rode and a short chain leader, aim for about 7:1 for a secure anchorage, and no less than about 5:1 for a short stop in calm conditions. If it's 10 feet from your bow to the bottom, that's 50 to 70 feet of rode.
  • With an all-chain rode, the weight of the chain helps, so less scope works; somewhere around 3:1 to 5:1 is common, more in a blow.
  • Let out more when the wind picks up.

Marking your rode at regular intervals makes it easy to know how much is out.

Setting the anchor

  1. Head slowly into the wind (or the current, if it's stronger) and stop the boat over the spot where you want the anchor to lie.
  2. Lower the anchor to the bottom from the bow. Don't throw it; it can tangle and land upside down.
  3. Let the boat drift or slowly reverse back as you pay out the rode, until you reach your scope.
  4. Cleat the line at the bow, then back down gently in reverse to dig the anchor in. The line should come tight and the boat should stop moving relative to the shore.
  5. Pick two landmarks on shore, or set the anchor alarm on your chartplotter, so you can tell if you're moving.

Never anchor from the stern

On a small boat, anchoring only from the stern is dangerous. The stern is usually the lowest part of the boat and has the motor's weight on it. With the anchor holding the stern into the waves, a wave or the wake of a passing boat can break over the transom and swamp the boat in seconds. Always anchor from the bow. If you need a stern anchor to keep the boat from swinging, set the bow anchor first.

Swing room and other boats

An anchored boat swings around its anchor as the wind and current change, in a circle whose radius is roughly the length of rode you've let out plus the boat's length. Leave enough room that you won't swing into other boats, docks or shallows, and remember that boats on all-chain and boats on rope swing differently. The first boat in an anchorage generally sets the pattern; anchor so you won't foul it.

Check it, and keep checking

Look at your landmarks or anchor alarm regularly, and more often if the wind picks up or shifts. Signs of dragging include a changing view of the shore, the depth changing, or the boat lying beam-on to the wind. If you're dragging, start the engine before you do anything else, then reset with more scope, or move.

From sunset to sunrise, an anchored boat must generally show an all-round white anchor light where others can see it. Check the navigation rules for your boat's size and location.

Getting the anchor back

Motor slowly toward the anchor while taking in the rode, so you're not dragging the boat up to it with the line. When you're directly above it, the anchor usually breaks free with a firm pull. If it's stuck, try motoring slowly past it in the opposite direction from the way it was set, with the line cleated short. Never cleat the anchor line to the stern to pull a stuck anchor free; that can capsize the boat. In snag-prone bottoms, a trip line tied to the crown of the anchor and buoyed at the surface lets you pull it out backwards.

The short version: anchor from the bow, in a sheltered spot with a good bottom, with about 7:1 scope on a rope rode. Back down to set it, take a bearing, check it often, and never anchor or pull from the stern.
